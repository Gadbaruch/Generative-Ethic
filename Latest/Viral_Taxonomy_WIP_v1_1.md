## The Core Question

The core document establishes that every quad state can propagate in either a contained or viral mode, and that the moral weight of viral propagation varies significantly depending on which quad is spreading. But it does not yet fully account for *how* systems go viral — the strategies through which propagation occurs, and what those strategies do to the systems they touch.

This document develops that question.

---

## The Viral Coefficient

Not all viral systems spread at the same rate or reach. The **viral coefficient** of a system describes how effectively it propagates its orientation to lateral systems it encounters.

The primary determinant of viral coefficient is **lateral usefulness**: how much does this system actually help other systems maintain their own structure? Systems that lateral systems find useful get adopted. Systems that don't, don't spread — or spread briefly and die off. A viral system that delivers no real usefulness to its lateral systems will not sustain propagation. The usefulness has to be real for the virality to hold.

This has a critical implication: **virality and goodness are orthogonal.** A system can deliver real, genuine internal usefulness to the systems within its basin and still be highly destructive — to systems outside the basin, to the broader emergence hierarchy, to the downward subsystems being consumed to fuel the spread. Dangerous viral systems are not spreading on false promises alone. They are spreading because they genuinely deliver something valuable to the systems they convert. This is precisely what makes them hard to resist.

The moral evaluation of a viral system therefore cannot rest on whether its usefulness is real. It must rest on what the spread does to the systems *outside* the basin and to the vertical layers above and below. This is developed fully in the section on Virality and Goodness below.

Within the internal usefulness driving virality, two components can be distinguished:

**Genuine utility** — the system improves the lateral system's adaptive capacity, Emergent Alignment, and generative potential. The converted system becomes more capable and more itself.

**Sensation of utility** — the system produces the feeling of value without building real capacity. The sensation is real — the signified value is not. Sensation utility can produce faster initial spread than genuine utility because it is cheaper to produce and easier to receive. It creates dependency rather than capability.

These components are not mutually exclusive and most viral systems carry both in varying proportions. But they are not the primary moral discriminator — that role belongs to the system's external and vertical effects, not its internal utility profile.

## The Mechanism: Hook, Promise, Delivery

Viral propagation operates through a three-stage sequence that explains both how systems spread and why the spread can be sustained independent of whether the system is generatively good:

**Stage 1 — The Hook:** Creates an unresolved tension in the lateral system — an open loop that demands closure. The hook captures attention by implying that resolution is available and near. It operates entirely on *perceived* usefulness: the expectation of a payoff, not the payoff itself. The Zeigarnik effect — the brain's compulsion to resolve open loops — is the underlying cognitive mechanism. A compelling hook does not need to deliver value. It needs to make value feel imminent.

**Stage 2 — The Promise:** Specifies the form the payoff will take. The promise communicates what kind of usefulness the adopting system will receive — security, belonging, meaning, pleasure, survival, knowledge, identity. The strength of the promise determines how strongly the lateral system is drawn toward adoption. Different lateral systems respond to different promises, which is why multi-strategy viral systems deploy multiple hooks targeting different entry points simultaneously.

**Stage 3 — The Delivery:** What actually arrives after adoption. This determines whether virality is sustained, fades, or locks in through dependency. Delivery is where the moral character of the viral system becomes structurally visible — not in the hook or the promise, which can be identical for Emergent and Rigid systems, but in what the lateral system actually becomes through adoption.

The critical asymmetry: stages 1 and 2 drive initial virality and are entirely about perceived usefulness. Stage 3 determines long-term character and is where real versus sensation utility diverges. Rigid viral systems succeed partly because they optimize stage 1 and 2 independently of stage 3 — and then develop lock-in mechanisms (dependency, exit cost, existential pressure) to prevent stage 3 from being evaluated clearly.

**The practical implication:** You cannot evaluate the moral character of a viral system from its hook or its promise. You can only evaluate it from what it does to the systems it converts — and from its effects on systems outside its basin and on the broader emergence hierarchy. This is why the unknowability principle bites hard for viral systems: the hook is always visible, the promise is always audible, and the delivery always takes time.

## Usefulness as the Engine of Attractor Formation

The preceding sections treat virality primarily as a propagation dynamic — how systems spread. This section addresses a deeper structural question: what is the relationship between virality and the formation of higher-level emergent systems?

The proposed answer: **mutual usefulness between lateral systems is the primary engine by which higher-level emergent systems form, and the attractor that mutual usefulness creates defines the boundary of that higher-level system.**

The sequence:

1. Separated lateral systems exist independently
2. One system finds a way to provide genuine usefulness to another — reducing its resource expenditure, increasing its stability, improving its function
3. The receiving system reciprocates, providing usefulness in return
4. This mutual usefulness creates a stable interaction pattern — both systems tend toward this configuration
5. That stable interaction pattern becomes an **attractor** — a state the system dynamics tend toward from multiple starting conditions
6. Other lateral systems that can benefit from similar mutual usefulness are drawn into the attractor's basin
7. The **basin boundary** — the region of all systems drawn toward this attractor — defines and constitutes the higher-level emergent system

The higher-level system IS the attractor's basin. Its boundary is defined not by geography or formal membership but by where mutual usefulness reaches. Systems inside the basin are organized by the attractor's dynamics. Systems outside the basin are not.

**Examples:**

*Trade networks* — separate communities find mutual usefulness in exchange. Stable trading patterns become an attractor. The basin (all communities drawn into the exchange network) IS the economic system. Its boundary is defined by where mutual usefulness extends.

*Multicellular organisms* — individual cells develop mutual metabolic usefulness. The stable exchange pattern becomes an attractor. The basin = the organism boundary. The organism is not a container but an attractor whose basin is defined by which cells are organized by mutual metabolic dependence.

*Cities* — people and specialists find mutual usefulness in proximity and specialization. The urban attractor forms. The basin = the city boundary. A city is not a place — it is an attractor defined by the reach of mutual usefulness between its lateral systems.

*Scientific communities* — researchers find mutual usefulness in shared findings, peer review, and cumulative knowledge. The collaborative attractor forms, its basin defining the community boundary.

**The moral distinction between attractor types:**

The kind of attractor formed — what holds systems within the basin — determines the moral character of the higher-level system:

*Genuine mutual usefulness → open attractor.* Systems that enter the basin become more capable, more generative, more themselves. They could exit but don't need to — participation is self-reinforcing because the usefulness is real. The basin expands naturally. The higher-level system is Emergent.

*Sensation utility → dependency attractor.* Systems that enter the basin feel better but become less capable of independent function. Exit becomes progressively harder not because it is coercively prevented but because dependency has degraded the capacity to function outside the basin. The higher-level system is Rigid.

*Existential utility → coercive attractor.* The basin boundary is maintained by making exit fatal rather than by the pull of mutual usefulness. The higher-level system is Rigid maintained by force.

**The key test:** What holds systems within the basin — genuine mutual enhancement, dependency, or coercion? This is the structural question that reveals the attractor's moral character independent of how compelling the hook was or how real the internal usefulness feels to basin members.

**Connection to viral coefficient:** The viral coefficient of a system is the rate at which its attractor basin expands — how quickly and how broadly it draws new lateral systems into mutual usefulness relationships. A high viral coefficient means the attractor is compelling to many different lateral systems from many different starting conditions. Whether that compellingness is based on genuine mutual enhancement or on dependency/coercion is what determines the moral quality of the spread.

What follows is a first-pass map of viral expansion strategies. These are not mutually exclusive — most successful viral systems deploy multiple strategies simultaneously, and multi-strategy virality is probably more stable than single-strategy virality.

---

### Strategy 1: Genuine Utility Propagation

**Mechanism:** The system spreads because it demonstrably improves the function of systems that adopt it. Adoption is chosen, not coerced. Each converted system becomes more capable, including more capable of further genuine propagation.

**Effect on converted systems:** Adaptive capacity increases. Emergent Alignment improves. The converted system becomes more generative in its own right — not dependent on the viral system but enhanced by it.

**Sustainability:** High. Self-reinforcing. The more systems that adopt it, the more evidence accumulates of its genuine value, and the more capable those systems become of propagating it further.

**Quad profile:** Emergent viral. The only strategy that constitutes viral goodness in the full sense.

**Examples:** The scientific method, writing systems, germ theory, democracy's core error-correcting mechanisms, double-entry bookkeeping, certain open-source software ecosystems.

**Key question still open:** How do you distinguish genuine utility from high-quality sensation of utility before conversion? The converted system often can't tell from inside. The test may only be visible in the long-run effects on adaptive capacity — which the unknowability principle suggests cannot be fully assessed in real time.

---

### Strategy 2: Sensation of Utility Propagation (False Utility)

**Mechanism:** The system spreads by producing experiences that feel like value — coherence, belonging, certainty, pleasure, identity — without building the real adaptive capacity that genuine utility would. The sensation is real; what it signifies is not.

**Effect on converted systems:** Dependency increases. The converted system requires continued input from the viral system to maintain its felt sense of function. Adaptive capacity degrades over time while subjective experience of coherence may remain high or increase.

**Sustainability:** Medium. Requires continued delivery of the sensation to maintain conversion. If supply of sensation stops, converted systems may recover — which is why Rigid viral systems tend to develop mechanisms that prevent exit.

**Quad profile:** Rigid viral in most cases. The mechanism of mimicry — producing the sensation of Emergent connection while suppressing the differentiation that would make it real.

**Examples:** Addictive substances, certain social media optimization loops (sensation of connection without substance), propaganda systems that produce certainty without accuracy, ideological systems that provide identity at the cost of critical capacity.

**The mimicry problem:** Sensation of utility is structurally indistinguishable from genuine utility at the moment of adoption. This is why it's the most dangerous viral strategy — not because its effects are immediately worse but because it's undetectable early and self-reinforcing once established. The converted system loses the adaptive capacity it would need to evaluate whether the conversion was genuine.

---

### Strategy 3: Existential Utility (Convert or Die)

**Mechanism:** The system spreads by making non-adoption existentially costly. The lateral system doesn't adopt because it finds the system useful — it adopts because refusal to adopt means elimination. The value proposition is maximal: the most compelling offer possible is survival.

**Effect on converted systems:** The converted system survives but under top-down constraint that suppresses its own Emergent Alignment. It persists, possibly even proliferates, but as a node within the conquering system's Rigid structure rather than as a generative system in its own right. Its adaptive capacity is redirected rather than built or destroyed.

**Sustainability:** Variable. Requires ongoing enforcement capacity to maintain conversion, which is resource-intensive. More sustainable if the coercive conversion develops secondary genuine or sensation utility elements over time — giving converted systems reasons to maintain adoption beyond fear.

**Quad profile:** Rigid viral primarily. The converted system's internal function is captured and reprogrammed into service of the conquering system's expansion.

**Examples:** Conquest-based empire expansion, cancer's relationship to neighboring healthy tissue (which it co-opts through signaling), certain regulatory capture dynamics where non-compliance is made prohibitively costly.

**The cancer analogy:** Cancer does not purely destroy — it converts. Neighboring healthy cells are reprogrammed through chemical signaling to support the tumor's growth. They don't die; they become complicit. This is existential utility at the cellular level — convert or be eliminated, and the conversion preserves the cell's existence in a form that serves the conquering system.

**Multi-strategy note:** The most durable convert-or-die systems historically develop genuine or sensation utility components over time. The Roman Empire began as largely coercive conquest and developed genuine utility (law, citizenship, infrastructure, trade) as pure territorial expansion became unsustainable. The switch from existential to genuine utility was the moment it became a civilization rather than just an expanding army. Islamic expansion historically combined all three strategies simultaneously — genuine utility in legal systems and knowledge preservation, sensation utility in identity and community, existential utility in conquest contexts. Multi-strategy virality is probably what made it one of the fastest-spreading systems in history.

---

### Strategy 4: Network Lock-In

**Mechanism:** The system spreads because the cost of non-adoption increases as more laterals adopt it. Not necessarily useful in isolation — increasingly necessary as it saturates the environment. Adoption becomes individually rational even if the collective outcome is degenerative.

**Effect on converted systems:** Function becomes dependent on the network, not on the system's own adaptive capacity. The converted system may actually become less capable of functioning independently even as it functions better within the network.

**Sustainability:** Very high once critical mass is reached. Self-reinforcing through path dependence — the more systems adopt, the more costly non-adoption becomes, regardless of the system's inherent value.

**Quad profile:** Variable. Network lock-in is a propagation mechanism, not a quad in itself. It can amplify Emergent, Rigid, or Destructive systems — whichever reaches critical mass first. This makes it particularly dangerous when combined with Rigid content, because the lock-in mechanism is quad-neutral.

**Examples:** Dominant technology platforms, certain currency systems, path-dependent technical standards (QWERTY keyboards), language dominance.

**Key distinction from genuine utility:** Network lock-in can persist and expand even when the underlying system is objectively worse than alternatives. The network effect overrides quality evaluation. This is a case where viral coefficient is high but decoupled from actual utility — a warning sign for Rigid viral dynamics.

---

### Strategy 5: Mimicry

**Mechanism:** The system spreads by resembling something genuinely useful closely enough that it is adopted before the difference becomes apparent. It exploits the pattern-recognition capacity of lateral systems — the same capacity that identifies genuine utility also identifies convincing imitation of it.

**Effect on converted systems:** Dependent on what the mimicking system actually does once adopted. May transition into sensation utility, existential utility, or reveal itself as genuinely useful (false mimicry, where the system turns out to be better than expected).

**Sustainability:** Medium-low unless combined with lock-in or existential utility. Once the difference from genuine utility becomes apparent, mimicry-based adoption tends to reverse — which is why mimicry-based Rigid systems develop enforcement mechanisms to prevent evaluation.

**Quad profile:** Rigid viral most commonly. The mimicry problem — the most dangerous form of degenerative connection is structurally indistinguishable from generative connection at the moment of adoption.

**Examples:** Viral Rigid systems that present as Emergent, cults that present as communities, certain financial instruments that present as stable assets, ideological systems that adopt scientific language without scientific methodology.

---

### Strategy 6: Consumptive Expansion

**Mechanism:** The system grows by directly incorporating lateral systems into itself — consuming their matter, energy, or adaptive capacity and adding it to the consuming system's own mass or resources. No new propagating nodes are created. The consuming system gets larger as a single node rather than more distributed as a network.

**Distinction from viral:** This is the key question the core conversation raised. Consumptive expansion is *not* viral in the strict sense. Viral propagation creates new nodes that carry the pattern forward. Consumptive expansion grows the original node without creating new propagators.

**Effect on consumed systems:** Elimination or complete absorption. The consumed system ceases to exist as a distinct entity.

**Sustainability:** Inherently limited. Requires constant access to fresh substrate. Once the immediate lateral environment is consumed, expansion stalls without a secondary strategy:
- *Territorial expansion* — continue moving into unconsumed substrate (locust swarm model)
- *Managed extraction* — consume at a rate slow enough for partial regeneration (requires developing Rigid elements to regulate consumption)
- *Switching to conversion* — at some point, converting substrate into productive nodes becomes more efficient than consuming raw substrate. Most large-scale Destructive systems eventually develop viral elements for this reason.

**Quad profile:** Destructive. Not viral in itself, but frequently develops viral secondary strategies to sustain expansion.

**The entropy convergence:** Pure consumptive expansion without any secondary strategy — no conversion, no managed extraction, no territorial expansion — converges on the same endpoint as pure separation in the paradox of the extremes. Everything consumed, nothing converted, no propagating nodes, uniform nothingness. Maximum Destructive expansion is thermodynamically indistinguishable from entropy.

**Examples:** Wildfire (consumptive, not viral — burned areas don't become fires), certain purely extractive colonial models, black holes as a physical analogy, entropy itself as the limiting case.

## Multi-Strategy Virality

The most historically significant and durable viral systems combine multiple strategies simultaneously, adapting their mix as circumstances change.

A system that deploys:
- Genuine utility for those who can recognize it
- Sensation utility for those who can't
- Existential utility for those who resist
- Network lock-in once critical mass is reached
- Mimicry of whatever the target population already values

...is structurally very difficult to resist and very difficult to displace once established. The Roman Empire, major world religions, and dominant technology platforms all share elements of this multi-strategy profile.

The moral evaluation of multi-strategy viral systems is complex precisely because the strategies are inseparable in practice. A system that offers genuine utility alongside existential coercion cannot be cleanly credited with the genuine utility while being condemned for the coercion. The mix matters. The dominant strategy matters. And crucially — which strategy the system *relies on* to maintain its propagation, versus which strategies are auxiliary, matters most.

**The test:** If you removed the existential utility component — if adoption became genuinely optional — would the system still spread? If yes, the genuine or sensation utility is load-bearing. If no, the coercive component is load-bearing and the genuine utility is window dressing.

---

## The Viral Coefficient Revisited

Given the strategy taxonomy, the viral coefficient of a system is not a single number but a weighted combination of its strategy mix. A system with high genuine utility coefficient + high sensation utility coefficient + network lock-in is likely to spread faster and more durably than one relying on existential utility alone, which requires expensive enforcement.

The most important moral variable is not the total viral coefficient but its **composition**: what proportion of the coefficient comes from genuine utility versus other strategies? And what does the spread do to the converted systems' own adaptive capacity?

**High genuine utility coefficient → viral goodness.** The spread builds capacity in the systems it touches. Each converted system becomes a better propagator of genuine value.

**Low genuine utility coefficient, high other strategies → viral Rigid or viral Destructive.** The spread extracts from or captures the systems it touches. Each converted system becomes a weaker independent agent and a more dependent node.

## The Fundamental Correction: Virality and Goodness Are Orthogonal

The most important structural insight in this document, and the one that most sharply distinguishes the framework's account of virality from naive intuition:

**Virality does not imply goodness. Goodness does not imply virality. They are structurally independent.**

A system can be highly viral — spreading rapidly, building robust attractors, drawing in many lateral systems — while being highly destructive to the broader emergence hierarchy. And a system can be genuinely good — building real adaptive capacity, contributing to upward emergence — while spreading slowly or not at all.

The error is to conflate the two because viral goodness (genuine utility propagating rapidly) is the ideal we are working toward. But the ideal is not the rule. Most highly viral systems in history have not been unambiguously good. Many of the most generatively good developments in human history spread slowly against significant resistance.

**Why dangerous systems sustain virality:**

Dangerous viral systems spread because they deliver real internal usefulness to the lateral systems within their basin. Not promised usefulness. Not imagined usefulness. Real usefulness, experienced by real systems, which is precisely why they are adopted and maintained.

- Cancer delivers real resources and replication capacity to cancer cells
- Totalitarianism delivers real security, certainty, order, and identity to many of its subjects
- Addictive systems deliver real relief from pain and real pleasure
- Exploitative ideologies deliver real belonging, real community, real meaning

None of these are spreading on false promises alone. The internal value is real. The problem is not the internal value — it is what the system does with its spread.

**The moral evaluation lives in the external and vertical effects:**

| Axis | Question | Determines |
|---|---|---|
| **Internal usefulness** | Does the system deliver real value to systems within its basin? | Virality — not goodness |
| **External lateral effect** | What does the basin's expansion do to systems *outside* it? | Primary moral evaluation |
| **Upward effect** | Does the higher-level system constituted by this attractor contribute to or degrade the broader emergence hierarchy? | Primary moral evaluation |
| **Downward effect** | Are the constituent systems of the attractor being built up or consumed to fuel the spread? | Primary moral evaluation |

The moral verdict comes from the external lateral, upward, and downward axes — not from the internal usefulness axis.

**The critical test for viral goodness:**

A viral system passes the goodness test when: **systems outside the basin become more capable as a result of the attractor's existence — not just systems inside it.** The attractor creates positive externalities. Its expansion builds adaptive capacity in the broader system, not only in its own members.

A viral system fails the goodness test when: the basin expands at the expense of external systems — depleting their adaptive capacity, crowding out alternative attractors, degrading the emergence hierarchy above. The internal value is real; the external damage is also real. The net effect on the broader system is what matters.

**Practical implications:**

You cannot evaluate a viral system's goodness by observing how useful it is to its members. Cancer cells thrive. You must observe what the system's expansion does to everything outside its basin.

You cannot evaluate a viral system's goodness by how compelling its hook is. The most dangerous systems often have the most compelling hooks — because the internal usefulness they deliver is genuinely high.

You can evaluate a viral system's goodness by asking: does the world outside the basin become more or less generatively capable as this attractor expands? Does the broader emergence hierarchy gain or lose adaptive capacity? Do alternative attractors flourish or get crowded out?

These are the questions the framework's anti-authoritarian safeguards are designed to keep asking even when the internal experience of the viral system is positive, coherent, and meaningful — which it usually is, for everyone inside it.

## The Parasitization Problem: A Contention with the Brave Choice

A genuine and unresolved challenge to the framework's recommendation of viral goodness as a moral obligation.

**The structural argument:**

Viral goodness, by succeeding, creates exactly the conditions parasitic systems require. An open, trusting, resource-rich, generative system — which is precisely what viral goodness produces — is the ideal substrate for extraction. The more successful the viral goodness, the more attractive the target. Openness is simultaneously the source of viral goodness's strength and its structural vulnerability. Parasitic systems have a competitive advantage in this dynamic: they don't bear the cost of building capacity, only of extracting it. They can free-ride on the generative infrastructure that viral goodness creates.

The historical pattern is consistent enough to be structurally concerning:

- The internet spread genuine utility and was rapidly colonized by surveillance capitalism, misinformation, and algorithmic extraction
- Liberal democracy spread genuine utility and became a target for authoritarian capture through democratic mechanisms
- The scientific revolution spread genuine utility and developed a Rigid institutional crust partly as a response to commercial and political extraction
- The Roman Empire spread genuine utility across three continents and was progressively hollowed out by parasitic extraction from within

The concern: viral goodness may necessarily create the conditions for its own parasitization. And if parasitic systems are faster-spreading than generative ones — because they don't bear the overhead of actually building capacity — then in the medium term, viral evil may consistently outcompete viral goodness even if the long-run trajectory still favors goodness.

This is not a reason to abandon the brave choice. It is a reason to hold it with honest awareness of what it costs.

---

**Response 1 — Testing strengthens the immune system (and systems need to be tested):**

The co-evolutionary dynamic may be not merely tolerable but necessary. A viral goodness system that has never faced parasitic challenge is actually more vulnerable than one that has been tested and survived — because the immune response is built through exposure, not through clean conditions. The immune system develops its capacities precisely by encountering and defeating pathogens. A system that has only ever operated without challenge has never had to develop or exercise its defensive mechanisms. When the parasitic challenge eventually arrives — and it will — the untested system is less equipped to respond.

This reframes the parasitization problem partially: challenge is not a side effect of openness to be minimized but a necessary feature of sustained health. Systems that survive parasitic pressure become more robust. Systems that never face it remain fragile. The brave choice to go viral is also a choice to enter the testing environment — and that testing, while costly, may be structurally necessary for genuine Emergent maturity.

This does not make parasitization harmless. It means the damage is not purely waste — it is also pressure that drives adaptive development in what survives.

---

**Response 2 — Contained goodness is safer, not inferior:**

The brave/safe distinction the framework already establishes applies directly here. Contained goodness is the safe choice — it does not attempt viral propagation, does not create parasitic opportunities at scale, and exerts positive influence through the quality of its existence rather than through active spread. This is genuinely good. It is not the cowardly or lesser choice.

The brave choice to go viral is riskier and potentially more generative — but it is not more obligatory. The framework already says: contained goodness is safe goodness and should not be diminished. Viral goodness is brave goodness and carries proportionally higher risk. Neither is definitively superior to the other. The parasitization problem strengthens the case for the safe choice being a legitimate and honorable path — not a consolation prize.

---

**Response 3 — The immune system is baked into the definition of goodness:**

The most structurally clean response. The framework's definition of Emergent goodness already requires: maintaining differentiation, preserving adaptability, enabling error-correction, protecting dissent. These properties — encoded in the anti-authoritarian safeguards — are structurally identical to the immune system against parasitic capture. They are not an add-on to viral goodness but constitutive of it.

This means: a viral goodness system that has been successfully parasitized — that has lost its dissent, its reversibility, its diversity — has not been corrupted from outside. It has drifted from the Emergent quad from within. Parasitic capture and loss of Emergent Alignment are the same event described from two angles. The parasite did not destroy the immune system; the loss of immune function is what allowed the parasite to establish itself.

The implication: maintaining the properties the framework already defines as goodness IS maintaining the immune system. There is no separate obligation to defend against parasitization — there is only the ongoing obligation to remain genuinely Emergent. If that obligation is met, parasitic capture is structurally resisted as a consequence of the definition. If it is abandoned, parasitic capture is not an external attack but a symptom of internal drift.

This does not make viral goodness invulnerable. It makes the vulnerability precisely legible: the moment a viral goodness system begins suppressing dissent to maintain coherence, it has already begun the transition that makes capture possible.

---

**The open question that remains:**

Even granting all three responses, the concern is not fully resolved. Systems that are genuinely Emergent can still be overwhelmed by parasitic pressure that is faster, more numerous, and better resourced than the immune system can handle. Response adequacy is not guaranteed by structural commitment. A system can be genuinely trying to maintain Emergent Alignment while being consumed by parasitic dynamics it cannot outpace.

The brave choice to pursue viral goodness therefore carries an irreducible risk that is not resolved by any structural argument — only accepted with clear eyes. The framework's own unknowability principle applies: you cannot know in advance whether your viral attempt will outpace the parasitic response it inevitably generates. The brave choice remains a wager. The parasitization problem is one of the things being wagered against.

*This contention is held open. It does not defeat the brave choice — but it is part of what makes it brave.*

- **The measurement problem:** How do you assess the external and vertical effects of a viral system from inside it? The internal experience is always positive for basin members. The external damage is often invisible from inside the basin by design. The unknowability principle bites hardest here.
- **Reverse virality:** Can a system have negative viral coefficient — actively reducing its own propagation? Is contained goodness a form of reverse virality or simply zero virality? Is there a case for deliberately anti-viral goodness?

- **The attractor competition question:** When multiple attractors compete for the same lateral systems, what determines which basin expands? Hook strength, delivery quality, or network effects? Does the answer differ between Emergent and Rigid attractors?
- **Can an attractor be genuinely Emergent internally and Rigid externally simultaneously?** A system that builds real capacity in its members while crowding out all alternative attractors in the broader system. If so, how do we evaluate it overall?
