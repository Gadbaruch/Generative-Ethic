# What Project Omega Doesn't Yet See

### Bobby Azarian's learning universe is one of the most serious attempts to ground meaning in science. It's missing a single ingredient — a way to tell good complexity from bad — and the gap shows up most where it matters most.

---

### A serious project, met seriously

Bobby Azarian has spent a decade building something genuinely ambitious: a picture of the cosmos as a system that *learns*. On this account the universe is not simply running down toward heat-death disorder. It is also running *up* — building atoms, then cells, then organisms, then minds, then cultures — because pockets of structure can grow more complex by exporting their disorder elsewhere. Complexity is the arc. Mind — or at least the model-bearing, predictive structure that mind is built from — is something the universe does more of, not less; what that structure ultimately *is*, and whether "consciousness" is the right name for it, is a separate question this essay doesn't need to settle. And the apparatus he reaches for to describe this — inference, prediction, the minimizing of long-run surprise — is real science with real reach.

Project Omega is the applied arm of that theory: the claim that if we understand the principles by which reality complexifies, we can act on them and move ourselves toward a better future. The instinct behind it — that a scientific account of the universe should also be able to tell us how to live in it — is exactly right, and rarer than it should be.

What follows is not a take-down. It's an attempt to name the one ingredient the project hasn't added yet, because once you see the gap you start to see it doing quiet work in several places — and the work it does is heaviest precisely where Bobby reached hardest: human conflict.

### The ingredient that isn't there yet

The Unifying Theory of Reality is a powerful description of *how* the universe builds complexity. What it doesn't yet contain is a way to say *which* complexity is worth building.

It has an arrow — the cosmos trends toward more structure, more organization, more mind. What it's missing is a scale of better and worse to lay against that arrow. It can describe. It can't yet evaluate.

That sounds like a philosopher's quibble until you try to *act* — and acting is the whole point of Project Omega. To act is to choose between options, and choosing requires a criterion for which option is better. Where does the theory get one? From the arc itself: align yourself with the direction the universe is already going. But that move quietly does two things it shouldn't.

First, it slides from "the universe tends this way" to "therefore you ought to push it that way." A description of how things go contains no instruction about what you owe; the *ought* has to come from somewhere the description can't supply. Second — and this is the more practical trouble — "complexity" alone can't choose for you, because the bad outcomes are complex too. A cancer is complex. An arms race is complex. A totalitarian bureaucracy is intricate, highly organized, and exquisitely structured. The arc tells you the universe makes more structure. It doesn't tell you which structures are alive and generative and which are elaborate forms of death. For that you need a separate criterion, and the theory doesn't have one yet.

This gap is the founding problem of a framework I've spent the last few years building — the [Generative Ethic](https://generative-ethic.gadbaruch.com). Its starting claim is that the obligation can't be *derived* from the cosmic arc, only freely *wagered* on; and most of its work goes into building the missing criterion for telling living order from dead order. I won't import its machinery here. I'd rather show what the missing ingredient costs — because the cost is clearest in the one place Bobby brought his cosmology down to a concrete human problem.

### Cost one: harmony that drifts toward erasure

Bobby's prescription for our divisions is a single unifying worldview — everyone coming to see themselves as part of one planetary whole, until the line between "us" and "them" stops doing its damage. He's careful to say this preserves the world's variety of cultures and customs, and I take that intent seriously. But look at the mechanism he proposes: reinterpret the major religions until what they *share* rises to the surface and their differences come to read as barriers to be transcended.

By his own earlier and very good insight, a worldview is precisely the thing that distinguishes one human group from another — it is the unit of difference. Dissolving worldviews into a shared common core is therefore not quite the preservation of difference he's after. It drifts, almost against its own intentions, toward homogenization — toward one spot everyone is asked to stand on — and calls the standing-together harmony.

There's a real version of what he wants, and it runs the other direction: many traditions staying fully themselves, in close contact, each becoming *more* articulate about what it is rather than gently sanded toward a common middle. But you can only aim there if you can tell which differences enrich the whole and which genuinely corrode it. That's the missing criterion again. Without it, the most reliable path to peace the frame can find is the one that flattens everyone onto the same point — and flatness is not the same thing as unity.

### Cost two: it has trouble seeing a wall that protects

Once every difference is a candidate barrier, every boundary starts to look like an obstacle to dissolve. But some separation protects. A membrane is what keeps a cell alive. A young institution needs a wall around it before it can survive contact with everything at once. A community under threat sometimes needs a border more than it needs an embrace.

The frame has essentially one category for a wall — obstacle — so its instinct is to take them down. In a real conflict that isn't a neutral recommendation; it can quietly disarm whichever party most needed the wall to keep existing. A theory that can't yet tell a prison wall from an immune system will tend to counsel removing both, and they are not the same thing.

### Cost three: the hardest case — an agent organized around an ending

This is the gap I'd most want Bobby to close, because it's where the cosmology meets its hardest test.

In this picture there are two basic things: connection, which is good, and barriers, which are to be overcome. There isn't yet room for an *agent* whose whole identity is foreclosure — one organized, coherently and on purpose, around the elimination of someone else. Destruction here tends to appear as a *byproduct*: of competition, of scarcity, of people failing to recognize their common humanity, of faceless administration.

You can feel the strain in his reading of Israel-Palestine. He frames the parties as caught in a competition neither side chose, and locates much of the fault in bureaucracies rather than in any actor's actual aims. Wherever genuine, willed destructiveness enters the story, the frame has trouble holding it — not from any want of moral seriousness, but because the theory simply hasn't built a place to put it. A destroyer, understood as a thing with its own identity and its own project, doesn't yet have a slot in the ontology.

And this matters more than it might seem, because an inclusive frame with no place for an active destroyer can be exploited in a specific way. Ask a system to dissolve every boundary and include everyone, and the one agent that is organized around elimination has an unusually easy path in — the openness that is the frame's great virtue is also, undefended, its opening. A worldview built to heal the world has to be able to perceive the parts of the world that don't want to be healed; otherwise its generosity can be turned against it. This is the place I think the project most needs reinforcement before it meets a real adversary — not because the generosity is wrong, but because generosity without a way to recognize bad faith is fragile in exactly the moments that count.

### Why this isn't a refutation

None of this makes Project Omega less worth doing, and the thing it hasn't built yet is bound up with the thing that gives it force. The drive to include, to unify, to act on the whole system at once is exactly what lends the project its energy and its reach — and its underlying aim, moving civilization toward something better, is one I share. The gap is narrower and far more fixable than it sounds: *better* is carrying enormous weight in that sentence, and the theory hasn't yet said what it means. Bobby has the arc. What he needs is the scale.

The scale is buildable — it's most of what my own framework is for: a criterion for telling generative order from the elaborate dead kind, a way to get from "the universe goes this way" to "so I will too" that is an openly chosen bet rather than a smuggled command, and enough structure to recognize a protective wall when it sees one and to name a destroyer as a destroyer. Those happen to be the precise three capacities the missing ingredient restores.

### The one correction

The arc is real — the universe does trend toward more structure, more integration, more of the model-bearing complexity that minds are built from. That's the agreement, and it isn't a small one. What follows from it is a wager, not an order — you're invited to bet on the direction, not commanded into it. And any philosophy that wants to harmonize humanity has to be able to see the things in humanity that don't want to be harmonized: the differences worth protecting, the walls worth keeping, and the rare agent organized entirely around an ending.

Project Omega already sees most of the board. The missing ingredient is just what brings the rest of it into view — and a niche that's filling up is a far better place to stand than one you reached first and alone.
