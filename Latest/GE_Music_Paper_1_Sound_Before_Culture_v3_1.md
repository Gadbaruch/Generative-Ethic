## The Thesis in One Paragraph

Sound is the communication language of nature. Long before there were animals, there was thunder, wind, rain, and the deep sonic signatures of geological force — sounds carrying information about the state of the environment that any organism capable of hearing could read. When animals evolved, they did not invent sonic communication from scratch. They entered a world already speaking, inherited its acoustic logic, elaborated it into their own vocalizations, and became active participants in an ever-changing orchestral arrangement that preceded them by billions of years. What humans call music is the cultural elaboration of this ancient substrate. And what human cultures did with it — the participatory musical rituals at the center of every religion, every political movement, every community that has ever held together across generational time — is the answer to the question that puzzled Yuval Harari: how did a medium-sized primate with no exceptional physical capabilities become the dominant organism on Earth, capable of cooperating in groups of millions? The answer is not language alone. Language is one part of the answer, and an important one. But among the most foundational parts — the one most consistently present across every culture, most physiologically direct in its mechanism, and most capable of operating at scale — is music. This paper makes that case.

## I. The Sound of the World

### 1.1 The Living Orchestra

Before there were ears, there was sound. The planet produces it continuously and has done so for billions of years: geological, atmospheric, hydrological. Volcanoes. Thunder. Wind moving through terrain. Water striking rock, falling, flowing. The ocean in different moods. Ice breaking. Fire crackling in dry vegetation.

These sounds carry information — not propositional information, not sentences, but something more fundamental: information about the *state* of the environment and the forces operating within it. The low, massive rumble of distant thunder carries different information than the sharp crack of a lightning strike close by. The gentle sound of rain on still water carries different information than the roar of a flash flood. Any organism with auditory apparatus can read this information, and reading it correctly has always been a matter of survival.

When living organisms evolved, they did not encounter this sonic environment as a passive backdrop. They evolved *inside* it, shaped by it, dependent on interpreting it correctly. And then they became active participants in it. Every species that produces sound — every insect, every frog, every bird, every mammal — adds its voice to what is, in ecological terms, a vast improvised orchestral arrangement: ever-changing, always layered, never the same twice. The dawn chorus of a rainforest is not a collection of isolated signals. It is a living acoustic ecosystem in which hundreds of species simultaneously produce sound, each in relation to the others, the whole constituting an ambient information field that every organism in that forest is continuously reading.

This is the original context of music. Not the concert hall, not the cathedral, not the stadium — the living forest, the savanna at dusk, the river at night. Every human musical tradition is an elaboration of something that began here, in the co-participation of living organisms in a shared acoustic world they did not make but that made them.

**The crucial implication for the thesis:** animals do not merely *react* to the acoustic environment. They are *constituted* by it — their perceptual systems, their emotional responses, their social behaviors are shaped by millions of years of evolving inside this orchestra. When humans make music together, they are activating perceptual and emotional systems that were shaped by this ancient participation. The depth of music's effect on human psychology is not an accident of culture. It is the inheritance of an organism that evolved inside a singing world.

### 1.2 The Acoustic Alphabet: A Critical Qualification

Within this orchestral environment, a consistent acoustic alphabet emerged — a set of relationships between sound properties and environmental or emotional states that appear to be conserved across vertebrate taxa. This alphabet forms the physical foundation of all animal and human musical communication.

The basic parameters:

**Low frequency + broad bandwidth + high amplitude = threat/danger/dominance.** This is the growl of the predator, the roar of the storm, the rumble of the earthquake, the distorted roar of a metal guitar. Research by E.F. Briefer and colleagues (2013, *Frontiers in Psychology*) documents this pattern across animal taxa: "Low, broadband sounds are often honestly tied to body size and hostile intent, and can induce fear in receivers." A dog growling and a distant thunderstorm are activating the same response circuits in the mammalian nervous system.

**High frequency + narrow bandwidth + moderate amplitude = safety/affiliation/invitation.** The tonal birdsong, the mother's voice pitched high to an infant, the gentle flute, the clear bell. "High-pitched tonal sounds are related to appeasement, and are often produced to reduce fear in listeners."

**Rhythmic regularity = predictability/safety.** The metronome quality of a steady rain, a regular heartbeat, a walking pace — rhythmic regularity signals that nothing is about to change violently. Disrupted, irregular rhythm signals unpredictability and potential threat. Speed of rhythmic regularity also matters: a slow, regular pulse carries a different emotional signature than a fast one, even at the same volume — the slow pulse suggesting something large and steady, the fast pulse something small and urgent.

**Synchronized group sound = collective presence and power.** When many organisms produce sound together, the synchronized output signals coordinated collective presence. The group is announcing itself as a coherent entity: *we are here, we are together, we are coordinated.* This signal has weight on both sides — toward competitors (this group is cohesive) and within the group itself (we are together).

**A critical qualification.** These parameters cannot be applied uniformly across all species without significant caution, and it is important to state why. Every animal species has its own frequency range of hearing, its own temporal resolution, and its own perceptual relationship to the acoustic world. What sounds "high-pitched" to a human is not perceived in the same way by a bat operating in ultrasound, or an elephant communicating in infrasound, or an insect whose entire auditory world occupies a spectral band inaudible to us.

More strikingly: birds process sound at a much higher temporal resolution than humans — approximately 2 milliseconds compared to around 20 milliseconds for humans, roughly ten times finer. The bird is hearing the world in slow motion: each moment arrives with far greater temporal granularity, more "frames per second" of acoustic experience. We are the ones hearing it sped up. What a bird perceives as a temporally rich, detailed event — a complex sentence of information — arrives in our auditory system as a brief compressed chirp, with most of the detail smoothed away. The "simple melody" we hear is a lossy, accelerated compression of something far richer. A bird call that sounds to a human like a single brief melodic phrase may contain, at the bird's temporal resolution, information about individual identity, physical condition, local group membership, and emotional state that we have no capacity to parse.

This has a profound implication: animals with a faster lifestyle are equipped with perceptual systems that can detect changes in the environment at higher rates. High temporal resolution may come at a cost of increased energy expenditure, as it requires faster neural processing. Each species inhabits, in a genuine sense, its own temporal frame — and what constitutes "fast" or "slow," "high" or "low," is relative to that frame.

The acoustic alphabet described here therefore applies most reliably *within* groups of similar-sized mammals with overlapping auditory ranges and similar temporal resolution — which is the context of most cross-species emotional communication research, and the context most relevant to human musical experience. Claims about cross-species emotional communication must be understood as operating within this constraint. They are not claims that all species read the same sounds identically, but that the *structural* relationships between acoustic parameters and emotional states — the directional logic of the alphabet — appear to be conserved at a level that transcends specific frequency ranges. The hypothesis is that what a bat hears as "large and threatening" may map onto different absolute frequencies than what a human hears as "large and threatening," but the *structural relationship* — low-relative-to-range, broad-bandwidth, high-amplitude — maps onto threat across taxa.

This remains partly a hypothesis. The full story of cross-species temporal and spectral perception is a genuinely open scientific question, and one that deserves its own dedicated investigation. What we can say with confidence is that the structural logic of the acoustic alphabet is cross-species documented — and that within the range of mammals most relevant to hominid evolution, the evidence for emotional communication through shared acoustic parameters is substantial.

### 1.3 Sonic Niches: Finding Your Frequency in the Orchestra

One of the most elegant demonstrations that the orchestral arrangement of nature is not metaphor but ecology is the documented phenomenon of **acoustic niche partitioning**: species evolving to occupy specific frequency bands, temporal windows, and acoustic profiles that allow their signals to be heard and recognized against the background noise of other species.

In a dense rainforest, where dozens of species are vocalizing simultaneously, the pressure to occupy a distinct acoustic niche is intense. A mating call that is acoustically identical in frequency range and timing to a dominant neighboring species will be masked — received as noise rather than signal. Over evolutionary time, species partition the acoustic space: some occupy high frequencies, some low; some call at dawn, some at dusk, some at night; some use rapid staccato patterns, some long sustained tones. The result is a structured acoustic community — not the random cacophony it might seem from outside, but an organized system in which each species has found, through evolutionary pressure, the acoustic position from which it can most effectively communicate.

Animals that need to attract mates or coordinate group behavior across a dense jungle have evolved not arbitrary vocalizations but *acoustically strategic* ones: the frequency range that carries farthest through that specific environment, the temporal pattern that is most recognizable against that background, the timbral quality that distinguishes their species from its neighbors. The nightingale's extraordinary vocal range and complexity is not merely decorative — it is an evolutionary response to competition for acoustic niche in a crowded sonic environment.

This reframes the relationship between organisms and the acoustic world. Animals do not merely *use* sound. They have been shaped by the acoustic ecology they participate in, have evolved to occupy niches within it, and constitute that ecology as active participants. The "orchestra" is not a metaphor for something else. It is the actual structure of acoustic life in a living ecosystem — and every species in that ecosystem is simultaneously a musician (producing sound), an audience (receiving and interpreting others' sound), and a composer (shaped by evolutionary pressure to find its unique voice within the arrangement).

Human music, on this view, is what happens when the most acoustically sophisticated organism in the history of life consciously reflects on and elaborates the orchestral logic it inherited, and turns that elaboration into culture.

## II. Animals Elaborate the Language

### 2.1 Collective Vocal Synchrony Across the Animal Kingdom

When animals evolved, they inherited the acoustic alphabet that physical processes had established, and they elaborated it into increasingly sophisticated social signaling. The elaboration of *collective* synchronized vocalization — multiple animals producing sound together — is found across taxa so distant from each other that it cannot be explained by common ancestry at the species level. It converges independently because it solves the same problem: how do you signal collective presence, cohesion, and coordination?

Insects: firefly chorusing, cricket synchronization, katydid group calling. Frogs: the coordinated night chorus. Birds: the dawn chorus, the complex duet singing documented in wrens where male and female alternate syllables so precisely they sound like a single bird. Cetaceans: whale song, which shows regional cultural variation suggesting transmission across generations. Wolves: the howl chorus, which serves no individual alarm function but appears primarily to affirm collective group cohesion.

And crucially — our closest living relatives. A landmark 2025 study in *Current Biology* (Eleuteri, Hobaiter, Ravignani et al.) analyzing 371 drumming bouts across 11 chimpanzee communities in five African countries demonstrated that wild chimpanzee drumming shows structural musical features — non-random timing and isochrony — and regional variation between subspecies: chimpanzees and bonobos, like humans, drum on instrumental substrates. This is not incidental behavior. It is rhythmic percussion with individual and regional stylistic variation — what in human music we would call *cultural* variation. As the researcher Vesta Eleuteri noted: "As our closest living relatives, chimpanzees offer a good opportunity. If we find shared elements in their communications or behavior, we can at least say, maybe, that this capacity already existed in our common ancestor. And later, we may have incorporated this same capacity into our music."

Bonobos show spontaneous rhythmic entrainment to external beats (Large & Gray, 2015) and documented cases of synchronized bipedalism — coupled whole-body rhythmic entrainment between two individuals in what researchers describe as ritualized dance-like behavior. Social bonding drives their vocal exchanges: bonded males tend to chorus together, matching each other's pant hoots — the most parsimonious explanation being that it functions to reinforce social bonding, facilitating mate identification and reunion, joint territory defense, and social integration.

The critical observation, which the chimp and bonobo data confirms: the capacity for rhythmic synchrony, individual stylistic variation, and social bonding through collective sound production predates Homo sapiens. We did not invent this. We inherited it — and then took it further than any other species has.

**Addressing the skeptic:** The question sometimes raised is whether music is necessary for binding even in small ancestral groups, given that hunter-gatherer bands shared a gene pool and were, functionally, extended families. The chimp evidence makes the most direct answer: even our closest relatives, who also live in kin-bonded groups, display rhythmic and vocal behaviors that function for social cohesion *in addition to* kinship. Kinship creates the group; collective sonic practice maintains and deepens the bonds within it. As the group scales beyond kinship — as the relevant social unit expands from the band to the tribe, the tribe to the nation, the nation to the global musical tribe — the sonic binding technology becomes progressively more load-bearing, not less. This is exactly the trajectory Harari traces, and music is the technology that made each step of scaling possible.

### 2.2 Darwin's Protolanguage and the Musilanguage Hypothesis

Charles Darwin, in *The Descent of Man* (1871), proposed that before language, hominids produced complex vocalizations that were partly musical and partly proto-linguistic — "musical tones and rhythms" used to express emotion and coordinate social behavior. Language and music then diverged from this shared ancestor as their respective functional demands developed. The proposal has been given rigorous contemporary form in Steven Brown's *musilanguage* hypothesis (*The Origins of Music*, MIT Press, 2000): both music and language derive from a common ancestral vocal system — the "musilanguage" stage of hominid evolution — characterized by pitch contour and rhythmic phrasing.

What followed from this ancestral system was not that music came *after* language as decorative elaboration. The elaboration of the human vocal apparatus — the fine motor control, the extended phonetic range, the breath control — was an adaptation serving both proto-musical and proto-linguistic functions simultaneously. Daniel Levitin makes the point directly: music "paved the way for more complex behaviors such as language, large-scale cooperative undertakings, and the passing down of important information from one generation to the next" (*The World in Six Songs*, 2008). Music built the social substrate on which language could operate at scale.

### 2.3 A Speculative Note: When Sound Became Word

*[This section is exploratory and should be treated as a hypothesis requiring further research rather than a settled claim.]*

If music and language share a common ancestor in vocalized emotional communication, it raises a compelling question: do the sounds that humans use outside of formal language — the pre-linguistic vocalizations that communicate without words — preserve traces of that ancestral system?

Consider the *shh* sound. Across virtually all human languages, some variant of this sound functions as a command to be quiet. It requires no translation. A parent uses it in Tokyo and in Nairobi and is understood. It is not a word in any language — it is a sound, carrying meaning purely through its acoustic properties: sustained, sibilant, gentle in volume. The word *shush* appears to be the elaboration of this pre-linguistic sound into a lexical form, with a percussive final consonant added. The meaning traveled from pure sound into language, carrying its acoustic logic with it.

The infant vocalization *ma* or *mama* is another candidate. Across many unrelated language families, the maternal referent is constructed from bilabial sounds produced naturally by a mouth opening and closing — the movement an infant makes while nursing. The hypothesis is that before this sound was a word, it was a sonic gesture: the mouth-sound of hunger and need, directed at the person who provides. The word may have crystallized around a pre-linguistic acoustic communication.

If words evolved from sound communications, then in their original form — before they were fixed as words — they were closer to music: tone, timbre, rhythm, and intensity carrying meaning before semantic content was attached. Language, on this view, is music that learned to be more precise. It gained the capacity to represent stable abstract concepts, to denote specific objects and actions, to construct logical relations. But it may have lost something in the trade: the directness, the pre-cognitive speed, the physiological immediacy of pure sonic emotional communication.

This is speculative, but it points toward a unity that the separation of "music" from "language" in Western thought has obscured. In many oral traditions, the distinction is not made. The Homeric bard did not separate poem from song. The Vedic chanter did not separate sacred text from sacred tone. The griot does not separate history from music. The distinction may be a later artifact — a consequence of writing, which can preserve semantic content but cannot preserve tone — rather than a reflection of how these two faculties actually relate in the human body.

## III. The Human Innovation: Building Cultures With Music

### 3.1 The Harari Problem — and Music as the Missing Link

In *Sapiens* (2011), Yuval Noah Harari asks what is perhaps the most important question in human history: why did Homo sapiens become the dominant organism on Earth and the only species capable of large-scale flexible cooperation? His answer is shared fiction — the ability of millions of people who have never met to cooperate because they share belief in the same gods, nations, legal systems, and money.

This is correct and important. But it is incomplete in a specific way. Shared narrative explains *what* holds human societies together at the cognitive level. It does not fully explain *how* the shared narrative gets into bodies rather than merely minds — how millions of people come to *feel* rather than merely *know* that they are part of the same thing.

The argument of this paper is that participatory music is among the primary technologies by which shared narrative becomes a felt physiological reality rather than a mere cognitive proposition. Music does not convince people to belong. It makes them *feel* the belonging, in the body, in the moment, before and beneath the level at which cognitive assent operates. The shared narrative gives the belonging content and direction. The music makes the belonging *real* — neurologically, biochemically, somatically.

Harari's sapiens built nations, religions, and civilizations on shared fictions. But the fictions only held because music was underneath them, making the belonging feel true in every body that participated in the ritual. Remove the music and keep the doctrine: the doctrine slowly loses its grip. Keep the music and lose the doctrine: a new doctrine grows from the belonging the music keeps alive. This is not speculation. It is the observable pattern of history.

Furthermore, music provides the specific mechanism by which Harari's own trajectory of expanding human cooperation was achieved at each stage. Harari describes human societies growing from bands of dozens to tribes of hundreds to kingdoms of thousands to nations of millions to the global system of today. At each stage of that expansion, the primary tool for extending the felt sense of belonging across a wider circle of strangers was participatory music. Rock and roll bonded teenagers across Cold War geographies. Hip hop created global tribal identity across race and continent. EDM created temporary communities of belonging for millions at a time with no shared language except the beat. These are not decorative accompaniments to the cultural globalization Harari describes. They are among its primary mechanisms — the technology by which belonging expanded from the local to the planetary.

### 3.2 Scaling the Group: Dunbar and the Endorphin Hypothesis

The specific evolutionary mechanism by which music enabled large-scale human cooperation has been proposed with increasing empirical precision by Robin Dunbar and colleagues. The baseline constraint on primate social bonding is this: bonding in primates operates through direct physical contact — grooming — and grooming is time-limited. One animal can groom one other animal at a time. The maximum group size maintainable through grooming-based bonding is approximately 150 individuals — the ceiling that primate social organization bumps against.

Human social groups routinely exceed 150 by orders of magnitude. Dunbar's proposal: group vocal activity — shared singing, chanting, rhythmic movement — produces endorphin release at a scale that physical grooming cannot match. You can sing with a thousand people simultaneously. You cannot groom a thousand people. The endorphin hypothesis has been empirically tested: group singing elevated pain thresholds (an indirect endorphin measure) more than solo singing, and this elevation correlated with increased self-reported social bonding. The implication: the biological mechanism that enabled Homo sapiens to exceed the primate social-bonding ceiling and organize at scale was participatory music-making.

### 3.3 Epic as Music: The Transmission of Culture

The oldest large-scale cultural transmission of which we have record was not written text. It was song.

Homer's *Iliad* and *Odyssey* — the foundational works of Western literary culture — were not written documents in their original form. They were performed. The *aoidoi* — professional bard-singers — sang the epics to live audiences, accompanying themselves on a four-stringed lyre-like instrument called the *phorminx*, improvising both the melody and textual variations in real time. Stefan Hagel of the Austrian Academy of Sciences, who has spent thirty-five years reconstructing Homeric singing technique, concludes that the Homeric bard "would improvise the melody at the same time as he improvised his text, which was unique in every performance." Most scholars are convinced that Homer stood in a tradition of lyre-accompanied epic song, just as depicted in both the *Iliad* and the *Odyssey*.

The epics were transmitted for generations — possibly centuries — through musical performance before being written down. The mechanism of cultural transmission was musical. The pattern is consistent across the oldest cultural traditions:

The **Vedas** — among the oldest religious texts in the world, dating to approximately 1500 BCE — were transmitted orally through an extraordinarily precise chanting tradition for centuries before being written. The **Quran** is recited in a form so musically precise that there is a formal science of correct recitation — *tajwid* — with rules governing pitch, rhythm, duration, and articulation. The **Torah** was chanted. The **Bible** was sung into existence: the Psalms are a songbook, and the earliest Christian communities sang their theology before they systematized it in writing.

And from the African continent, one of the most remarkable systems of musical cultural transmission ever documented: the **Izibongo** tradition of the Zulu and Xhosa peoples of southern Africa. An individual's naming song tells the story of who they are by recounting the lineage they come from — chanting back through the praise names of illustrious living and dead ancestors of the individual's family, clan, and people. In KwaZulu-Natal, praise singers called *izimbongi* carry entire family lineages in memory — reciting ancestors going back forty generations. You cannot burn what lives in a person's memory. You cannot confiscate a poem that exists only in breath.

Each individual's izibongo is unique to their ancestry — a personalized song that is simultaneously autobiography, genealogy, and ethical claim about the values one carries. Each generation adds to it, making it longer. The form grows with history, carrying the past forward in song. The term *izibongo* refers to the form of poetic expression that defines and names an individual, characterized by bold imagery in carefully selected language — a personal set of praise names and epithets, comprising cumulative series of praises bestowed by associates from childhood onward.

This is not decoration on cultural transmission. This *is* cultural transmission, in its most durable form: carried in the breath and memory of living people, requiring no institution, no archive, no writing system. As in the Jewish diaspora or the Homeric tradition, the music is the medium in which the culture survives when every other infrastructure is destroyed.

One further case brings this function into the modern era with particular clarity, and adds a dimension the other examples do not: music as *current events journalism*, operating in real time. **Calypso** music — originating in Trinidad and Tobago in the 18th and 19th centuries from enslaved West African communities — was not primarily entertainment. It was a living newspaper. Traditionalists see calypso as social commentary because in its earlier years it served the purpose of telling stories, relaying news events, and giving criticisms of persons and policy. The calypsonian was, in pre-literate plantation society, the medium through which news traveled: local political corruption, the challenges of daily life, neighborhood drama, colonial injustice — all delivered in song, in call-and-response form, to gathered audiences who carried the information and passed it on.

The lineage is direct. Calypso had its roots in the West African custom of griot court singing — the same tradition that produced the izibongo and the Homeric aoidoi, transplanted through the Middle Passage and continuing to function in new soil. The *chantwells* — local bards akin to West African griots — performed during Canboulay festivals, leading call-and-response songs in French patois to rally communities, mock authorities, and relay news and satire. The tradition survived under conditions of extraordinary suppression by adapting: embedding its messages in double entendre and coded language to evade censorship, preserving African polyrhythmic structure while developing a hybrid musical form suited to its new Caribbean context.

The colonial authorities understood the threat with perfect clarity. As they had done in Ireland with the bardic tradition, the British moved to outlaw the musical form: in 1884 they made it illegal to play African drums. You do not ban a newspaper by banning the printing press unless you understand that the printing press is the source of power. The drums were banned because the drums were the medium — and the medium was carrying information, solidarity, and resistance.

The pattern running through all these cases — Homer, the Vedas, the Quran, the izibongo, the calypso — is the same. Human cultures transmit their most important content through music first and text second. Music is not decoration on cultural transmission. It is the original vehicle of cultural transmission. Writing is the technology that later captured *some* of what music had been carrying — but only some. The emotional reality, the collective belonging, the felt knowledge that the content is *ours* and *true* — these were always carried in the music, not the text.

## IV. The Universal Pattern: Every Culture, Every Binding

### 4.1 The Claim

Every human culture that has sustained itself across generations — regardless of geography, theological framework, economic system, stated ideology, or level of technological development — has at its center a form of participatory musical ritual. Not passive listening. Not professional performance for audiences. Participatory: the group makes the music together, moves together to it, or synchronizes bodies in the production of it.

This is not coincidence. The thesis of this paper is that this universal pattern exists because participatory music is the technology by which the belonging that all durable cultures require is produced in human bodies — neurologically, biochemically, and somatically — in a way that no other technology can replicate. What follows is the evidence.

### 4.2 Hunter-Gatherer and Tribal Societies

The anthropological record of pre-literate and tribal societies shows participatory musical ritual as the central binding practice without exception. Drumming circles, collective chanting, ceremonial dance — documented across every pre-literate society for which we have anthropological record. They are not peripheral entertainments. They are the occasions on which group identity is constituted and renewed: coming-of-age ceremonies, harvest rituals, healing ceremonies, war preparations, grief rituals, seasonal celebrations.

John Blacking, whose work on the Venda people of South Africa remains foundational, argued in *How Musical Is Man?* (1973) that music among the Venda is a communal practice that defines communal life. Musical ability is not a gift given to some people; it is the natural capacity of social beings exercised collectively. His broader claim: "Music is a model of reality, and a means of creating it."

Patrick Savage and colleagues (2015) analyzed 304 recordings across 60 societies and found that music universally functions as a social technology, with features associated with social coordination cross-culturally consistent while melodic content varies enormously. The social coordination function is the invariant. The melodic content is the culturally specific elaboration.

### 4.3 What Music Teaches: The Curriculum Within the Ritual

One of the most underexamined functions of participatory musical practice is its role as what might be called **social simulation** — a structured experience through which a community rehearses and transmits the values, relational dynamics, and cognitive dispositions it considers essential to collective life. Music is not only the binding technology. It is also the classroom in which the rules of the binding are learned, internalized, and felt rather than merely stated.

Different musical traditions teach different lessons, and the lessons they teach are not accidental. They reflect the social structure and values of the cultures that created them. Consider what the specific formal properties of different musical traditions actually demonstrate to their practitioners:

**1. Unison as unity.** When a group sings or drums the same pattern together — the same beat, the same melody, the same rhythm — the experience is of collective power through identity. Everyone the same, moving as one. This is the lesson of the military march, the national anthem, the protest chant, the unison tribal drumming of many Native American traditions: *when we all do the same thing together, we are stronger than any one of us.* The felt reality of this truth is produced in the body of every participant every time the ritual is performed. It needs no verbal instruction.

**2. Ensemble as cooperation without uniformity.** In virtually all ensemble music that is not pure unison — the string quartet, the jazz band, the Gospel choir singing soprano, alto, tenor and bass, the rock group, the Renaissance polyphonic mass, the West African drumming circle — each participant contributes something distinct, and the whole is richer than any individual part. No single player is playing "the music." The music is the relationship between the parts. The lesson is not specific to any tradition: *you do not have to be identical to your community in order to contribute to something meaningful. Everyone has their part. What matters is the shared organizing principle — the key, the pulse, the form — that holds the differentiated contributions together.* This is a more sophisticated social model than unison, and it is embedded in nearly every ensemble musical tradition on earth without exception. It is taught not as a proposition but as an experience: you feel, in the act of playing your part while hearing others play theirs, that differentiation and cohesion are not opposites. They are the same thing, properly understood.

**3. The invisible shared center.** A refinement of point two, found across many traditions: the organizing principle — the pulse, the tonal center, the shared form — is often not played directly by anyone. It is held internally by every member of the ensemble, each generating their contribution in relation to it, trusting that everyone else is doing the same. The music exists around a center that no one is expressing. A jazz musician playing against the beat is not ignoring the beat — they are *depending* on it, and on every other player depending on it, in order for the displacement to mean anything. The silence at the center is what makes the music cohere. The lesson is profound: *a community can be organized around a shared internal center — a value, a commitment, a way of being — that need not be externally stated or performed to be real and binding. What connects us can be invisible, carried inside each member, and the music exists around it rather than in it.* This is arguably the deepest musical expression of what the GE framework identifies as the ground of collective identity: not the visible ritual but the invisible shared orientation that the ritual moves around.

**4. Polyrhythmic transition as cognitive flexibility.** In performances that use overlapping meters — such as the experience of three-against-four (3:4) polyrhythm — something remarkable happens in the listener and player. The same sequence of sounds can be felt in two genuinely different ways: as organized in groups of three, or in groups of four. Both are valid. Neither cancels the other. A skilled listener can shift between the two frames of perception without the music stopping — can hold the music in one meter, then transition to experiencing it in the other, or can arrive at the state of holding both simultaneously. This experience — which is disorienting at first, then manageable, then finally pleasurable and illuminating — is, in effect, a training in cognitive flexibility: the ability to hold two genuinely different valid interpretations of the same reality at the same time. The musical tradition provides a *play environment* in which this capacity can be developed without the stakes of real social conflict. Cultures that build this experience into their musical practice may be producing members who have been trained, bodily and neurologically, for a capacity that is essential to complex social life: the ability to recognize that one's current frame of perception is not the only valid one.

**5. Classical hierarchy as social mirror.** In the Western classical tradition, a different set of lessons is embedded in the musical form. The conductor holds supreme authority — every musician subordinates their individual timing and interpretation to the conductor's direction. The orchestra is hierarchically organized: first desk, second desk, section principals, soloists who demonstrate individual virtuosity within the framework of collective discipline. Precision, control, mastery of self, and absolute coherence with the collective are prized above spontaneity. The soloist must demonstrate extraordinary individual excellence — but in service of the compositional whole, not against it. This is not incidentally the social model of the European societies that produced and valorized this musical tradition: hierarchical, meritocratic within the hierarchy, valuing individual excellence in service of collective order. The music does not describe the social structure. The music *is* the social structure, practiced in sound.

**6. Improvisation as the discipline of listening.** Group musical improvisation teaches something that no other musical form requires to the same degree: you cannot do it without continuously, actively listening to everyone around you. This sounds simple. It is extraordinarily difficult, and it is the opposite of what most social performance trains us to do.

In most social contexts — debate, negotiation, presentation, even conversation — a significant portion of attention is directed inward: planning what to say next, constructing the argument, managing self-presentation. The improvising musician cannot afford this. The moment attention turns inward and away from what is happening in the room, the musical contribution loses its coherence with the whole. It becomes noise rather than music — not because it is technically wrong but because it is no longer *responsive*. It is no longer in genuine relation.

The discipline of group improvisation is therefore, at its core, a training in presence — in the radical reorientation of attention from self-production toward collective reception. You produce, but production is downstream of listening. You contribute, but contribution only makes sense as response. The music teaches, through direct physiological experience and immediate audible feedback, that *the quality of what you create is a function of the quality of your attention to others.* A musician who stops listening in order to play more will play worse. A musician who listens more deeply will play better — even if they play less.

This lesson extends far beyond music. It describes the fundamental condition of any genuinely generative collective process: the quality of the collective output depends on the quality of each member's attention to the whole. Communities, institutions, and relationships that produce something greater than the sum of their parts are ones in which the members are genuinely listening — not waiting to speak, not executing pre-formed plans, but tracking what is actually happening and responding to it in real time. Communities that produce less than the sum of their parts are usually ones in which members have stopped listening to each other.

Group musical improvisation is a practice environment for exactly this capacity — perhaps the most ancient and the most effective one that exists. This is the point where music-as-social-simulation opens toward something larger: the possibility that improvisation is not merely a musical technique but a model of how generative collective consciousness operates. That larger argument belongs to a companion paper; what can be said here is that the discipline of listening — the specific, physiologically demanding, immediately consequential listening that improvisation requires — is one of the most important things music has ever taught, and one of the things most at risk of being lost when participatory music is replaced by passive consumption.

The general principle that emerges from these six observations is this: **societies create musical languages and rituals that simulate the values and social structures they wish to transmit to their members.** Music is not decoration on culture. It is the practice environment in which culture is internalized — where abstract social values become embodied habits of perception, attention, and relation. And like all practice environments, it can be used to cultivate capacities that are genuinely generative, or to reinforce patterns that are limiting. The analysis of a culture's musical practices is therefore, among other things, an analysis of what that culture is actually teaching its members to be — as distinct from what it says it is teaching them.

**A connection point to the GE framework — held as live hypothesis.**

The claim that music functions as *social simulation* connects to a deeper claim in the Generative Ethic's Extended Metaphysics that deserves to be stated here, even if it cannot be fully developed within the scope of this paper.

The GE's Fractal Consciousness Hypothesis proposes that consciousness, at any scale, is the computational signature of a navigating thread: a system that builds internal models of its own trajectory, uses those models to select among possible continuations, and acts in ways not fully determined by prior causal state. Critically, the hypothesis holds that this structure is *scale-invariant* — the same connection/separation dynamic appearing fractally at every level of the emergence hierarchy, from the individual organism to the collective. Individual human consciousness is a simulation of the generative structure of reality running at the scale of one body. A society, when it achieves genuine collective navigation — real-time mutual modeling, coupled error-correction, coordinated response to shared conditions — may instantiate something structurally analogous at the scale of many bodies.

Music as social simulation fits into this framework conservatively: a practice environment in which the collective develops the habits, values, and relational patterns it needs to simulate its situation more accurately and navigate it more generatively.

But the stronger claim is possible, and the neural evidence for it now exists. In group musical improvisation at its peak — where each participant is continuously modeling all the others in real time, responding to what they produce while contributing their own part, with the whole generating something no individual could have generated alone — the inter-brain synchrony documented in the hyperscanning research is not metaphorical. The brains are coupling. The individual simulations are merging, at least partially, into something that functions as a single distributed simulation process running across multiple bodies simultaneously.

If the Fractal Consciousness Hypothesis is correct — if consciousness is a simulation process that is genuinely scale-invariant — then the question is no longer merely philosophical: *is what happens in peak group musical improvisation a temporary instantiation of collective consciousness?* The structural description fits. Multiple navigating threads, each with its own internal model, coupling in real time into a coordinated whole that models itself and its environment more accurately than any thread could alone, producing outputs not determined by any single thread's prior state. This is what the GE identifies as the structure of a navigating system. The fact that it is temporary — that it dissolves when the music stops, that it does not yet have the institutional infrastructure to sustain itself between sessions — does not necessarily disqualify it. Individual consciousness also appears and dissolves; its continuity is partly a construction.

The more precise statement of the hypothesis is this: *group musical improvisation may be the most reliable human technology for temporarily producing the structural conditions of collective consciousness — not as a metaphor, not as a feeling, but as an actual functional instantiation of what the GE framework identifies as the computational signature of a navigating entity at the collective scale.* If this is correct, the stakes of participatory musical practice are not merely cultural or social. They are philosophical in the deepest sense: music may be one of the few human activities that reliably and reproducibly generates, however briefly, a genuinely higher-order form of mind.

This claim is held here as a live hypothesis, not a derivation. It requires the improvisation paper to develop properly. But it belongs here as a signal that the "social simulation" framing, while accurate, may be the floor of what music does — not the ceiling.

### 4.4 The Religious Traditions

**Vedic tradition:** The Rigveda — one of the oldest documented musical practices in human history, dating to approximately 1500 BCE — is a collection of hymns to be chanted in specific tonal patterns. The Vedic chanting tradition is explicitly communal and precisely specified: the correct production of the sound, collectively, was understood as having cosmological efficacy. The sound *was* the divine act.

**Judaism:** Shabbat songs, the Passover seder, cantorial liturgy, the communal Psalms — Jewish religious life is saturated with participatory musical practice. The seder is a musical event as much as a narrative one: the Haggadah is sung, not recited. The shared tunes of Shabbat table songs — known to every practicing Jew regardless of where in the world they were raised — create a trans-generational and trans-geographic web of belonging that has maintained Jewish cultural identity across two thousand years of diaspora, systematic persecution, and the absence of any continuous territory. The music carried the culture when nothing else could.

**Christianity:** Gregorian chant, the Lutheran chorale, Gospel, the entire Protestant tradition from Luther (who explicitly used congregational singing as a theological tool — "next to the Word of God, music deserves the highest praise") to contemporary worship music. The Gospel tradition in Black American Christianity is one of the most documented cases of music as collective binding under conditions of oppression — a direct line from the slave spiritual through Gospel to the freedom song of the Civil Rights Movement.

**Islam:** Quranic recitation is the paradigmatic sacred act — voiced, melodically precise, public. The Sufi traditions elaborate this: the *sama* (sacred listening session) and *qawwali* (devotional music) are explicitly designed to induce collective mystical states through sustained musical engagement. The *adhan* — the call to prayer — is a five-times-daily sonic event that synchronizes the entire community's attention.

**Buddhism and Hinduism:** Tibetan Buddhist chanting, Zen chanting, Hindu bhajan and kirtan — all are collective participatory practices understood as transformation rather than performance. The kirtan tradition in particular — call-and-response devotional singing that can continue for hours — is a technology for producing experiential dissolution of self-consciousness into collective sound.

### 4.5 Calvin's Congregation: Intensifying Participation

A case that confirms the thesis from an unexpected direction. Calvin suppressed *professional musical performance for passive audiences* and replaced it with something more powerful: universal mandatory participation.

The Genevan Psalter (1562) gave every congregant all 150 Psalms in metrical form, to be sung in unison without instrumental accompaniment. Calvin specified in 1537: "children... sing in a loud distinct voice, the people listening with all attention and following heartily what is sung with the mouth, till all become accustomed to singing communally." No instruments. No choir performing for the congregation. Every voice. For English Protestants in exile under Mary I, the metrical psalms maintained group identity under conditions of displacement and persecution — precisely the conditions that test binding most severely. Calvin understood, instinctively, what this paper argues theoretically: the passive audience is not a community. The singing congregation is.

### 4.6 The Contemporary Secular Equivalents

**The stadium.** Tens of thousands synchronizing chant, song, and rhythmic movement around a shared object of collective attention. The intensity of belonging produced — willingness to identify with strangers, genuine collective distress at loss, genuine collective joy — is not explained by the game alone. It is produced by the participatory musical ritual: the synchronized chanting, the anthems, the rhythmic movement.

**The protest march.** Drums at the front. Chants repeated until every body is in synchrony. The body moving to the rhythm of hundreds of others. The chant rising from individual voices into a single collective sound that seems to come from something larger than any individual producing it. Every significant mass political movement in living memory has used this structure.

**The rave and the club.** An accidental rediscovery of ancient technology — but an incomplete one, and the incompleteness is instructive. The specific architectural features of the rave — darkness reducing visual differentiation between individuals, bass frequencies felt somatically, continuous rhythm driving physiological entrainment, collective physical movement — constitute a genuine environment for producing collective states. The reported experiences are real: unity, dissolution of normal social boundaries, felt belonging, contact with something larger. These are not illusions. The mechanism is operating.

But notice what is absent: there is no musical *participation*. Nobody in a club is making the music. The music is produced by one person — the DJ — and received by everyone else. The crowd dances, but does not sing, does not play, does not co-create the sound. Compared to the stadium singing its anthem or the congregation singing its psalm, the rave sits lower on the participation gradient — closer to collective rhythmic response than to genuine collective musical production.

The dancing itself mirrors this. In most contemporary club and rave contexts, people dance individually — each person moving in their own way to the shared beat, bodies proximate but not physically coordinated with each other. Nobody is holding hands. Nobody is moving in deliberate synchrony with their neighbor. The shared beat provides a common pulse, but within it each person is largely alone, expressing their individual response to the music rather than merging their movement with others'. This is not necessarily worse — there is something honest and even valuable in the individualism of it, the permission to move as you move without conforming to a choreography. But it is worth naming: the rave may be, inadvertently, a musical ritual that teaches and reflects the core value of the society that produced it. Everyone shares the pulse. Within it, you are on your own. The crowd is physically together and socially atomized — present with others without being genuinely connected to them. This is a reasonably accurate description of modern urban life more broadly.

The other significant feature of rave culture is the widespread use of substances — MDMA and psychedelics particularly — to deepen the experience of connection and dissolve the ordinary boundaries of self. This is worth examining without moral judgment, because it points toward something structurally important. MDMA produces its effects largely by triggering a massive release of oxytocin and serotonin — the same neurochemicals that group singing activates, but through a pharmacological override of the natural pathway rather than through behavioral activation of it. The feeling of love, connection, and belonging that MDMA produces is, at the neurochemical level, not fundamentally different in kind from what a sustained Gospel service or a kirtan produces through collective musical participation. The mechanism is the same molecule. The pathway to it is different.

And the difference in pathway matters enormously. When the oxytocin release is triggered by active collective participation — by making music together, by synchronizing breath and voice and movement with other bodies — the release is gradual, self-reinforcing, and leaves no deficit in its wake. The body's natural production capacity is activated, not depleted. What follows a great church service or a powerful communal singing session is typically integration: a residual sense of warmth and connection that persists into the days following, a strengthened feeling of belonging to the people you were with.

When the release is pharmacologically forced — when the molecule is flooded into the system at many times the level natural behavior produces — the system typically responds by downregulating its production capacity. What follows is the crash: the days of flatness, isolation, or despair that frequently follow MDMA use, which are at least partly the neurochemical system recalibrating after depletion. The belonging felt during the experience was real. The chemical that produced it was real. But the pathway left the system worse off for having gone through it, and the belonging does not persist.

The rave, read through this lens, is a society that has largely lost access to the natural technologies for producing collective belonging — participatory music, communal ritual, the regular activation of bonding neurochemistry through shared practice — and has replaced them with a pharmacological shortcut that produces the feeling without the sustaining infrastructure. It is not a condemnation. It is a diagnosis. The hunger being fed is real and legitimate. The technology being used to feed it is a substitute for something the culture is no longer providing in its natural form. The fact that it requires increasingly powerful chemical assistance to produce what a village choir or a church congregation produces through the simple act of singing together is, in itself, a measure of what has been lost.

## V. The Cultural Invariant

### 5.1 What This Pattern Means

The universality of participatory musical ritual across human cultures, when fully examined, is not a fact that requires explanation. It is a fact that explains things.

It explains how cultures hold together across time despite constant internal disagreement and generational discontinuity. People who cannot agree on theology will agree on the song. The song is doing more structural work than anyone, including its practitioners, has recognized.

It explains why cultures deprived of their other supports — territory, political authority, economic continuity — can maintain coherent identity across centuries. The Jewish diaspora maintained cultural identity across two thousand years because the Shabbat table songs, the seder, the liturgical chanting, the lifecycle ceremony music survived. The music was not decoration on the Jewish cultural project. It *was* the Jewish cultural project, in its most durable form. This is equally true of the izibongo that survived colonialism, apartheid, and forced displacement in South Africa: you cannot burn what lives in breath.

The invariant is the mechanism. The variable is the content. Every human culture that has achieved durable large-scale cohesion has done so by placing participatory musical ritual at its center. The specific melodies, scales, instruments, texts, and occasions differ across cultures. The function does not.

### 5.2 Music as the Primary Attractor

There is a bolder claim available here, and it is worth stating directly even though it cannot be fully proven and will strike many as counterintuitive. It is this: when people are drawn into and kept within the institutions that constitute their cultural life — the church, the political movement, the supporter club, the community gathering — the primary attractor may not be what they consciously think it is.

Ask a regular churchgoer why they attend and they will tell you: faith, community, meaning, the teaching. Ask a committed supporter why they go to the stadium and they will say: love of the game, loyalty to the club, shared passion with other fans. Ask a movement activist why they keep marching and they will say: the cause, the urgency, the need for change. These stated reasons are all real. But they may not be the primary mechanism doing the pulling.

The argument of this paper suggests a different answer, or rather an additional one that operates below the level of conscious motivation. What the church, the stadium, and the march all have that a theology seminar, a sports bar, and a policy briefing do not — what they have that produces the felt sense of genuine belonging rather than mere intellectual engagement — is the musical ritual. The singing, the chanting, the synchronized movement, the shared rhythm. This is what the body is actually responding to. This is what keeps people coming back week after week when the sermon was merely adequate, when the team lost badly, when the march achieved nothing visible. The doctrine fluctuates in its grip. The music does not.

This would make participatory musical ritual not merely one feature among many in these institutions but their primary attractor basin — in the language of complexity theory, the stable configuration in phase space that nearby states are drawn toward, the deep structure that gives the whole system its pulling power. The theology is content delivered by the attractor. The political platform is a message carried by the attractor. The team identity is a story organized around the attractor. Remove the attractor — strip the church of its music, silence the terrace, ban the march from its drums — and watch how quickly the institution loses its grip on the bodies of its members, even when the stated reasons for belonging remain entirely intact.

This is not a claim that content does not matter. The content carried by the attractor determines the direction of the binding — whether the belonging it creates opens toward the world or closes against it. But it is a claim about which element is primary. The content matters enormously for where the binding goes. The music is what makes the binding happen in the first place.

If this is correct, it has a striking practical implication: any institution that wants to create genuine and durable belonging among its members — as opposed to nominal or intellectual membership — needs to design its musical practice first and its doctrine second. Not because music is more important than meaning, but because music is the vehicle through which meaning reaches the body, and a meaning that has not reached the body has not yet truly taken hold.

## Conclusion: Sound Before Culture

Music did not emerge from culture. Culture emerged from music.

The sonic communication system that vertebrates have been using for hundreds of millions of years — the acoustic alphabet written first by volcanoes and thunder and read by every organism with ears — provided the substrate. Hominids inherited that substrate, elaborated it into collective vocal synchrony, and used that technology to build the large-scale cooperative social groups that distinguish human civilization from anything else in the biological world.

The cultures built on that foundation — the religions, the political movements, the nations, the artistic traditions — all reflect this origin. Every one of them has participatory music at its center, because every one of them requires, to survive, the continuous regeneration of felt belonging among its members. That regeneration requires not argument but experience. Not doctrine but ritual. Not the word read silently in private but the song sung together in public.

Harari asked how a medium-sized primate became the dominant organism on Earth. The full answer requires music as a central part. The ability to coordinate millions is not achieved through language alone. It is achieved also — and perhaps most reliably, most physiologically directly — through the sonic: the ability to put thousands of bodies in a room, or a street, or a field, and make them one thing — not metaphorically, not ideologically, but physiologically, neurologically, biochemically — through the act of making sound together.

This is what music is. Not entertainment. Not decoration. Infrastructure.
